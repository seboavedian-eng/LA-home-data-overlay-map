## 1. Buyer's agents' CMAs and NAR's RPR reports: where comps help, where they're lazy or misleading, and what agents say about RPR

### Takeaway
Buyers rate their agents highly overall (91% would use them again or recommend them, NAR 2025). The documented failures are specific and repeat across sources:
- unadjusted price-per-square-foot math
- wrong square footage on comps
- renovated comps set against as-is homes
- "cherry-picked" high sales
- anchoring offers to recent overbids

What RPR's valuation (the RVM) is worth is unclear. The only accuracy claims come from RPR/NAR. The criticism comes from appraisers and from agents' comments on old posts (2011–2014). I found no independent accuracy study.

### Cited Findings
**Strengths (trust, communication, value)**
- NAR 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers (transactions July 2024–June 2025, released Nov 2025): **91% of buyers would use their agent again or recommend them.** — [NAR press release](https://www.nar.realtor/press-releases/first-time-home-buyer-share-falls-to-historic-low-of-21-median-age-rises-to-40)
- A secondary write-up gives 92% satisfied (59% very, 33% somewhat). I couldn't check this against NAR's own document. — [Virginia Realtors summary](https://virginiarealtors.org/2025/12/08/key-takeaways-from-nars-2025-profile-of-home-buyers-and-sellers)
- The 2024 Profile had 88% of buyers saying they'd use their agent again or recommend them, but only about 21% actually used the same agent again. Stated loyalty is much higher than repeat use. — [Real Estate Today substack summary of NAR 2024](https://realestatetoday.substack.com/p/nars-2024-survey-of-buyers-and-sellers)
- 88% of buyers still use agents (NAR 2025). — [nowbam summary](https://nowbam.com/88-of-home-buyers-still-rely-on-agents-nar-2025-report-finds)
- When asked who they'd trust most on how much to offer, consumers picked a real estate agent (25%) or their own research (23%) well ahead of an AI tool (9%). LendingTree, 2,001 US consumers, July 2026. — [MPA Mag](https://www.mpamag.com/us/specialty/transformation/buyers-are-more-ready-for-ai-than-brokers-might-expect-survey-finds/589368); [LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/ai-housing-survey/)
- Consumer guidance tells buyers to ask for a CMA before any serious offer: "As a buyer: ask your agent for a quick CMA before any serious offer." — [estatepass.ai CMA guide](https://www.estatepass.ai/tools/cma/guide/) (vendor)

**Weaknesses: accuracy and quality of comps**
- **Wrong square footage.** A North Carolina Real Estate Commission case study found the listing broker's square footage on two comps was off by **7% and 6%**, and the appraisal built on those comps came in well below list. — [NCREC bulletin](https://bulletins.ncrec.gov/square-footage-errors-a-regulatory-affairs-division-case-study/) (regulator, primary)
- "Inaccurate square footage is the most common data error in a CMA. It throws off every price-per-square-foot comparison that follows." — [estatepass.ai](https://www.estatepass.ai/tools/cma/guide/) (vendor)
- **Price per square foot alone:** "a CMA report may only focus on square footage alone, which could make for a poor comparison." — [Zillow buying guide](https://www.zillow.com/home-buying-guide/comparative-market-analysis/). "Two homes with identical square footage can have vastly different values based on location, finishes, and layout." — same results set (vendor guides)
- **Condition ignored.** A fully renovated comp set against an as-is home with no condition adjustment can overstate value by **10–20%**. This figure is a vendor claim. — [iBuyer.com, How to Pull Comps (2026)](https://ibuyer.com/blog/how-to-pull-comps-on-a-house/)
- **Portal-sourced comps.** Zillow "doesn't know if the kitchen was remodeled last year or if the roof is leaking." Its comp list "doesn't filter for era, style, condition, or mechanical stack." — [Opendoor comps article](https://www.opendoor.com/articles/real-estate-comps); [iBuyer.com](https://ibuyer.com/blog/how-to-pull-comps-on-a-house/) (vendors with a stake)
- **Cherry-picking.** A Delmar (NY) brokerage example describes an agent's quote that ran high by keeping only the highest recent sales on the block, with "minimal or missing adjustments." — [McDonald Real Estate](https://www.mcdonaldrealestateco.com/delmar-split-comps-versus-agent-list-price/)

**Weaknesses: conflicts of interest**
- A Teamblind thread (tech workers, likely Bay Area/Seattle) describes a buyer's agent who routinely recommends bidding over list, anchored to one recent overbid sale rather than adjusted comps. One commenter says agents pushing overbids raises prices faster. The thread suggests checking the agent's advice against homes the buyer didn't bid on. One thread, anecdotal, no vote count seen. — [Teamblind: "agent pushes us to overbid"](https://www.teamblind.com/post/agent-pushes-us-to-overbid-18uyzh0y)
- Appraisal-gap risk: buyers sometimes waive the appraisal contingency to compete, then can't cover the gap. — [Opendoor, appraisal came in low](https://www.opendoor.com/articles/appraisal-came-in-low)

**RPR / RVM: what agents and appraisers say**
- *For:* RPR/NAR say the RVM uses MLS data on sold and for-sale homes and is more accurate than other AVMs (automated valuation models). — [Inman 2012](https://inman.com/2012/12/13/veros-adds-realtors-valuation-model-avm-lineup/amp); [RPR blog: AVMs and the RVM](https://blog.narrpr.com/news/avms-and-the-realtor-difference/). A Howard Hanna executive (2018) called the RVM "traditionally the more accurate" of RPR's two valuation products. — [RISMedia 2018](https://www.rismedia.com/2018/06/04/providing-value-competitive-market/). An agent testimonial says RPR "changed the accuracy of my pricing" (anecdotal).
- *Against:* An appraiser's blog estimates RPR value reports are accurate "no more than 20% nationwide." This is explicitly his opinion, with no data. He also says the comps often aren't truly comparable. — [AppraisersBlogs: "The RPR Epidemic"](https://appraisersblogs.com/online-valuations-vs-trained-appraiser) (undated in snippet)
- In comments on RPR's own blog (2011–2014), agents call AVMs inaccurate and confusing to clients. One ran comps suggesting about $209k against an RVM of about $250k. Another said the seller report showed agent-picked comps next to the subject *at the RPR estimate*, which made it unusable with clients. — [RPR blog: The RVM Ignites](https://blog.narrpr.com/news/the-rvm-ignites/) (dated, company-hosted)
- G2 review: not every broker shares data with RPR, which limits coverage. — [G2 RPR reviews](https://g2.com/products/rpr-app/reviews)
- A 2020s industry critique series also reviews RPR's broker/agent demo. — [Notorious ROB substack](https://notoriousrob.substack.com/p/reviewing-rpr-demo-part-2-brokers-and-agents) (not read in full)

### Inferences
- High agent satisfaction and the documented comp errors can both be true. Buyers judge the *relationship*. Most can't audit the *comps*.
- Most of the "lazy CMA" failures could be caught with public data: square footage checked against the assessor roll, condition, cherry-picked radius. That's an opening for a tool that shows its sources.

### Gaps
- **No Reddit verbatims or upvote counts** (reddit.com blocked). Recent r/realtors views on RPR could not be retrieved.
- No independent, current accuracy test of the RVM.
- Nothing found specific to California or LA on CMA quality.

## 2. Home inspectors: praise vs. complaints (missed defects, boilerplate, liability caps, cost, scheduling), plus LA/California specifics

### Takeaway
Inspections do find costly problems, especially when a sewer scope is added. But the best controlled test (Consumers' Checkbook, about 2018) found inspectors **as a group caught only about half of 28 known defects.** The most common buyer complaint is missed water damage and drainage problems. In California, contract clauses that cap liability at the inspection fee are **void by statute.** In LA, buyers are routinely told a general inspection doesn't cover the sewer lateral or the foundation, so those need separate specialists.

### Cited Findings
**Strengths (finding real problems)**
- An inspector found a sewer section hidden under a vapor barrier at a flipped house, per a Reddit post reported by Yahoo (about Sept–Oct 2026). Commenters disputed whether the pipe was still in use. — [Yahoo Lifestyle](https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/articles/home-inspector-spots-hidden-sewer-235100853.html)
- A buyer who skipped the general inspection still paid for a sewer camera scan. It found root intrusion and a failing connection, and the buyer closed with a credit. — [Wizroo blog](https://wizroo.com/why-we-waived-the-inspection-saved-money/) (single anecdote, 2026)
- Review-site pattern: the most common praise is thoroughness and clear explanations, with occasional claims like "saved us thousands of dollars." — [HomeAdvisor review](https://www.homeadvisor.com/write-a-review/24285471/); Redfin Openbook inspector pages (e.g. [Seattle](https://www.redfin.com/buy-a-home/openbook/home-inspection/seattle-bryan-d-mize-sp479338))
- A structural engineer's report "often quantifies the likely repair cost," which gives buyers a number to negotiate with. — [jedi.la, "One Inspection Is Never Enough" (Aug 2026)](https://jedi.la/2026/08/24/%F0%9F%94%AC-one-inspection-is-never-enough/)

**Weaknesses: accuracy and thoroughness**
- **Consumers' Checkbook undercover test** (about 2018; the PDF files are spring_2018). The test house had 28 known defects and 12 inspectors were hired. "As a group, they missed, like half the problems we thought were just too obvious to miss" (executive editor). 9 of 12 missed some roof damage, 8 missed rat droppings, 5 missed a leak under the sink, 4 missed ceiling water damage. Few looked at the roof up close, several didn't test every window or outlet, and some reports were "brief and cursory." — [CBS Chicago](https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/inspecting-the-home-inspectors/); [KOMO](https://komonews.com/news/consumer/dont-assume-all-home-inspectors-do-a-thorough-job-and-catch-all-important-problems); [Checkbook article](https://www.checkbook.org/delaware-valley-area/home-inspectors/articles/We-Got-12-Home-Inspections-and-Were-Astonished-at-How-Poorly-Many-of-the-Inspectors-Performed-7029) (page not fetchable)
  - *Disputed:* Structure Tech (an inspection firm) argues some of the items fall outside the inspection Standards of Practice, and that Checkbook's account of the method changed when he asked about it. — [Structure Tech response](https://structuretech.com/consumers-checkbook-response/)
- **Reddit misses, compiled by AOL** (the original threads couldn't be reached):
  - "By far, the most common issue that home inspectors miss involves water damage", including a basement that flooded twice in the first year
  - a 25-year-old roof never mentioned
  - a collapsed cast-iron sewer line
  - bad aluminum wiring
  - unpermitted renovations

  The number of users per point wasn't visible. — [AOL roundup](https://www.aol.com/articles/homeowners-reddit-share-issues-missed-003000753.html)
- **Boilerplate and liability deflection.** An Oklahoma family's report had 17 recommendations and 3 safety hazards but missed the problems they found after moving in. The inspector said: "We are not liable for latent defects." — [FOX23 Investigates](https://fox23.com/news/fox23-investigates-wagoner-family-experiencing-issues-with-home-following-inspection/article_098a855c-63f8-4ce5-a9b0-bdba5d63d733.html)
- **Scope gap.** A Southern California sewer company notes the general inspector covers drain pipes under the house, while "a Sewer Line Inspection company inspects the sewer line, which is not part of the drainage lines under the house." — [Redfin Openbook: Williams Sewer Line Inspectors](https://www.redfin.com/openbook/sewer-inspection/southern-california-williams-sewer-line-inspectors-llc-sp304924)

**Cost / value (LA)**
- LA sewer scope: $250–$500 per one LA buyer guide, which also notes "root intrusion from LA's aggressive tree root systems is common" and that original clay and cast-iron laterals have finite lives. Another LA source puts it at $200–$400. National range: $125–$500. — [Stephanie Younger, 5 inspections every LA buyer should know](https://stephanieyounger.com/the-5-most-important-home-inspections-every-la-buyer-should-know/); [AmeriSave 2026](https://www.amerisave.com/learn/what-is-a-sewer-scope-inspection-in-definition-costs-and-what-home-buyers-should-know)
- Sewer replacement in LA: $8,000–$30,000+, against $1,000–$10,000+ nationally. — same sources
- LA foundation repair: $2,500–$15,000 for slab or crawlspace work, $30,000–$80,000+ for hillside caissons, or $5,800–$19,200 "typical" per another source. Contractor marketing, inconsistent. — [Keystone Connect SoCal 2026](https://keystoneconnect.us/cost-guides/foundation-repair); [APLA Construction](https://aplaconstruction.com/foundation/)
- A foundation and structural evaluation is advised for hillside homes, visible cracks or sloping floors, and unretrofitted pre-1940s houses. — [jedi.la](https://jedi.la/2026/08/24/%F0%9F%94%AC-one-inspection-is-never-enough/)

**Speed / scheduling inside escrow**
- "A sewer scope you can't schedule until day 16" of the contingency leaves no time to get a plumbing bid. — [jedi.la (Aug 2026)](https://jedi.la/2026/08/24/%F0%9F%94%AC-one-inspection-is-never-enough/)

**Trust / liability (California)**
- Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§7195–7199: clauses limiting an inspector's liability to the cost of the report are void as "contrary to public policy." Higher fixed caps aren't expressly barred, and InterNACHI's California agreement uses 1.5× the fee. — [InterNACHI](https://www.nachi.org/limitation-agreement.htm); [ASHI Reporter](https://www.homeinspector.org/reporter-articles/managing-risk-your-pre-inspection-agreement-limitation-of-liability/)
- *Moreno v. Sanchez* (Cal. Ct. App. 2003): a written inspection contract does not shield the inspector from tort liability. — [FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1324303.html)
- Industry practice is to refund the fee only in exchange for a signed release. One ASHI example: a $4,600 demand ended with the court ordering a $300 refund. Jurisdiction unclear. — [InspectorPro](https://www.inspectorproinsurance.com/contracts-and-reports/release-of-claims-contract-to-limit-home-inspector-liability/); [ASHI](https://www.homeinspector.org/reporter-articles/home-inspection-contract-agreements/)
- Suing an inspector is possible if they "failed to perform certain checks" a capable inspector would have done. — [HomeLight](https://www.homelight.com/blog/buyer-suing-home-inspector/)

### Inferences
- The complaints cluster where a visual, non-invasive inspection is structurally weak: water and drainage, the sewer lateral, the roof's age, permits. Permit history and roof age can be checked from public records before an offer is made. That's an opening for a pre-offer data tool, as a complement to an inspector, not a replacement.
- Because of the California limits on liability caps, recourse against an inspector is somewhat better in CA than in states where fee-only caps are enforced. I found no data on how often CA buyers actually recover anything.

### Gaps
- I didn't get a sourced 2024–2026 figure for a general home inspection in LA. The $400–$700 range in the brief is **unconfirmed** here. [Noble Property Inspections](https://www.noble-pi.com/blogs/home-inspection-cost-los-angeles) covers it but couldn't be read.
- No Yelp or Google review theme counts (no aggregate data could be reached).
- No Checkbook retest after 2018 was found.

## 3. General AI chat assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity) for home and neighbourhood research

### Takeaway
Use is now mainstream: roughly 35–50% of buyers in 2025–2026 surveys, with ChatGPT the most used. Trust is falling (Cotality: 16% trust AI for finding a home in 2026, down 14 points). People praise AI for explaining documents they paste in, sorting inspection items by urgency, and giving a general feel for a neighbourhood. They criticise it for confident made-up specifics: invented comps and addresses, wrong HOA fees and rules, stale data, and school boundaries.

### Cited Findings
**Adoption and speed (strength)**
- Veterans United: 34% of prospective buyers used AI tools in Q1 2025 (n=899, March 3–17, 2025), with ChatGPT at 26%, Zillow natural-language search 16% and Gemini 15%. In Q2 2025 it was 45%: ChatGPT 27%, Gemini 17%, Meta AI 14%. Top uses were estimating monthly payments (41%) and virtual tours (36%). — [Veterans United AI homebuying survey](https://www.veteransunited.com/education/ai-homebuying-survey/) (taken from snippets, page blocked)
- 35% of respondents used ChatGPT or other AI for information, Gen Z 43%. — [National MI NextGen Homebuyer Report 2025 (PDF)](https://nationalmi.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Final-NextGen-Homebuyer-Report-2025.pdf)
- Zillow's 2026 Consumer Housing Trends: about half of recent buyers used AI, while 85% used an agent. Zillow notes this is reported behaviour, not evidence that AI improved decisions. — [Plot Daily](https://plotdaily.com/stories/zillow-half-of-homebuyers-used-ai-85-percent-used-agent)
- NerdWallet 2026 Home Buyer Report: 48% of prospective buyers say they will use AI. — [NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/mortgages/down-payment-reality-report)
- Bank of America: 1 in 5 prospective buyers or homeowners use AI in the homebuying process. — [Barchart/BofA](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/2603774/bofa-study-more-americans-favor-buying-over-renting-for-the-first-time-since-2023)

**Strengths: explaining, comparing, questions to ask**
- Realtor Ed Neuhaus's experiment (May 14, 2026) compared three Charlotte neighbourhoods and found it worked like a buyer consultation with no time limit. "If you paste in actual MLS data, actual tax records, actual HOA documents, ChatGPT can analyze them beautifully." — [neuhausre.com](https://neuhausre.com/chatgpt-buy-a-home-experiment/)
- A buyer uploaded their inspection report and had ChatGPT sort the findings by urgency. — [Recordly blog](https://www.recordlydata.com/blog/chatgpt-to-buy-house) (from a snippet)
- ASHI describes first-time buyers asking ChatGPT for the most serious problems instead of reading the report. ASHI's point is that how a report is structured affects the quality of the AI's answer. — [ASHI](https://www.homeinspector.org/how-agents-and-clients-read-inspection-reports-and-how-to-win-more-referrals/)
- ChatGPT was useful "for general neighborhood personality research." — [neuhausre home-value test](https://neuhausre.com/chatgpt-home-value-test/)

**Weaknesses: accuracy (hallucination, stale data)**
- "The wheels came off the minute I asked anything about actual listings, actual prices, or actual current data. ChatGPT had absorbed information from somewhere on the internet, probably a forum post from a few years ago, and was serving it back to me as current fact." It also called a home overpriced against comps it had invented. — [Neuhaus, May 2026](https://neuhausre.com/chatgpt-buy-a-home-experiment/)
- When asked for comps, "it will sometimes generate plausible-sounding addresses that don't exist." — [neuhausre home-value test](https://neuhausre.com/chatgpt-home-value-test/)
- A buyer-side test: the HOA fee was off by about $200 a month, and the short-term-rental rules ChatGPT gave didn't match the HOA's actual rules. — same results set ([neuhausre](https://neuhausre.com/chatgpt-home-value-test/))
- **School boundaries and zoning:** agent guidance says ChatGPT "does not know your MLS data, your lot lines, your school boundaries", so verify against official sources. — [HousingWire 2026](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/chatgpt-for-real-estate/); [Inman, May 2025](https://www.inman.com/2025/05/19/5-costly-mistakes-agents-make-with-chatgpt-and-how-to-fix-them/)
- Berlin apartment search (outside the US): the chatbot "helped accelerate apartment hunting even though it produced many errors." Bad suggestions included signing a lease without an in-person viewing. — [Yahoo Tech](https://tech.yahoo.com/science/articles/coder-used-chatgpt-apartment-berlin-141115976.html)
- 33% of Millennials and 36% of Gen Z think AI can produce incorrect information (Prosper Insights, 2026). — [Forbes, June 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/garydrenik/2026/06/23/why-ai-is-reshaping--not-replacing--the-real-estate-agent/)

**Trust**
- Cotality AI in Housing 2026: trust in AI to find a home is 16% (down 14 points from 2025). 68% would manually check a large share of AI output. Only 7% of Gen Z would accept AI information on property risk or insurance, and 56% would trust a human expert over AI on disaster risk. — [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/homebuyers-want-ai-and-human-in-the-loop-cotality-2026-survey/); [ProgramBusiness](https://programbusiness.com/news/trust-in-ai-declines-as-homebuyers-demand-greater-verification-cotality-finds/); [Inman, Apr 2026](https://www.inman.com/2026/04/21/homebuyer-trust-in-ai-is-slipping-survey-finds-video/)
- 37% would let AI complete a purchase with little human involvement and 41% would not (LendingTree, July 2026). — [LendingTree](https://www.lendingtree.com/home/mortgage/ai-housing-survey/)
- 85% of buyers use AI to research homes, and 81% still consider agents essential (AceableAgent 2026). — [Pillar blog](https://usepillar.com/blog/ai-tools-homebuyer-adoption-agent-demand/)

**Conflicts of interest / compliance**
- Calling a school district good or bad can count as steering under fair-housing law, so AI-written school claims carry legal risk as well as accuracy risk. This mainly applies to agents. — [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/chatgpt-for-real-estate/)

**Cost**
- No source found that weighs AI's cost (free to about $20/month) against agents or inspectors. Not cited.

### Inferences
- The strength (analysing documents you give it) and the weakness (inventing current local facts) point the same way. AI is most trusted when it works from cited, local, current data. Falling trust plus 68% wanting to verify suggests buyers want answers they can trace to a source. A map that cites each figure, like this project's `cardRow()` rule, fits that.
- Specific failures recorded so far: comps, HOA details, school boundaries. I found no documented cases about zoning or ADU rules.

### Gaps
- **No r/ChatGPT, r/RealEstate or r/FirstTimeHomeBuyer verbatims or vote counts** (Reddit blocked).
- No head-to-head comparison of Claude, Gemini or Perplexity for home research. All the anecdotes are about ChatGPT.
- No LA-specific AI accounts, and no documented case of a buyer harmed by wrong AI school or zoning information.

**Most useful and reliable sources for this kind of sentiment research** *(kept here as the section the assignment asked for)*
- **Most reliable:** primary surveys with stated methods (NAR Profile, Cotality AI in Housing, LendingTree, Veterans United quarterly, Zillow CHTR); regulator material (NCREC case study); statute and case law (Cal. B&P §7195+, *Moreno v. Sanchez*). Survey questions differ, so the AI-usage figures (20%–85%) can't be compared directly.
- **Best evidence on quality:** controlled tests like Consumers' Checkbook's 12-inspector study, though it's dated (about 2018) and disputed by the industry.
- **Best for verbatim sentiment:** Reddit, through press roundups (AOL, Yahoo) and practitioner first-person tests (Neuhaus). Reddit itself and most press pages were **unreachable in this sandbox**, so they need checking by hand.
- **Use with caution:** vendor and brokerage blogs (Opendoor, iBuyer, estatepass, contractor cost pages) supply useful mechanisms but come with a sales motive and inconsistent numbers. RPR's own blog is company-hosted, and its comment threads date from 2011–2014.
